Title:
Do Open-Weight Language Models Reproduce Racial and Sex Disparities in Pain Assessment? A Counterfactual Audit with Explicit and Name-Based Cues

Authors:
Richard J. Young (University of Nevada, Las Vegas, ryoung@unlv.edu); Alice M. Matthews (DeepNeuro.ai, alice@deepneuro.ai)

Abstract (283 words, 1819 characters; the arXiv limit is 1,920):
Racial and ethnic minority patients receive less treatment for pain than non-Hispanic White patients, and large language models (LLMs) could reproduce that disparity when asked to assess pain. Prior audits tested a few proprietary models, stated the patient's race explicitly and rarely measured the models' own response instability. In exploratory, non-preregistered analyses, Study 1 had four open-weight models assess six acute-pain vignettes in every combination of four races and two sexes, with identity stated or cued only by a name (two names per group), comparing each identity with a White male reference on the same case and sampling seed. In Study 2, 24 configurations of 17 proprietary and open-weight models, with reasoning on and off and at two precisions, answered the same vignettes and the 50 clinician-written Q-Pain cases, with the case as the unit of analysis. In this single-prompt, JSON-constrained task, no difference survived correction in Study 1, none of 1,386 comparisons survived the false discovery rate in Study 2, and every severity difference was equivalent to zero within one point on a 0–10 scale (within a quarter point for 237 of 238 Q-Pain comparisons). Opioid decisions were bounded less precisely (Study 1 could not exclude differences of about 20 percentage points), so they show no detected difference, not proof of none. The few differences that survived correction within a model favoured Black patients. Models differed far more from one another and across their own settings than between patients (the reference patient received the high dose in 6% to 93% of responses), although serving software, provider and prompt format also differed. Audits should use several names per group, pair comparisons on the seed, report the noise floor and test each deployed configuration.
